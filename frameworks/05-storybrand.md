# Donald Miller's StoryBrand

**Author/Origin:** Donald Miller, StoryBrand LLC, 2017
**Source:** *Building a StoryBrand: Clarify Your Message So Customers Will Listen* (Donald Miller, 2017)
**Type:** Dual-mode (macro for marketing and customer-facing content; micro for customer sections within larger frameworks)
**Best used for:** Any customer-facing communication — website copy, sales decks, email nurture sequences, product marketing, and brand messaging. The framework for making your message clear by making the customer the hero instead of the brand.

---

## Origin and Context

Donald Miller is a writer and CEO of StoryBrand LLC. He developed the framework by applying the principles of narrative screenwriting — specifically the story structure of Joseph Campbell and Robert McKee — to the problem of brand messaging. His observation was that most company communications fail not because the product is bad, but because the company makes itself the hero of the story. Customers do not buy products to help the brand succeed; they buy products to help themselves succeed.

*Building a StoryBrand* (2017) sold over one million copies and established StoryBrand as one of the most widely adopted marketing frameworks in English-speaking business. The book argues that confusion kills sales — customers who cannot quickly understand how your product helps them will not engage. Clarity, not cleverness, is the goal.

The framework has 7 stages. A common simplification reduces this to 6 by collapsing the final two stages ("Avoiding Failure" and "Achieving Success") into one fork. This is incorrect. In the full framework, both endings are always present — the brand shows what success looks like AND what failure (the cost of inaction) looks like. They are not alternatives; they are two sides of the same motivational coin.

The most distinctive contribution of StoryBrand is the three-layer problem structure. Miller argues that brands typically only address external problems (the practical, surface-level need). But humans are motivated by internal problems (the emotional or identity-level pain) and philosophical problems (the belief about how the world should work) far more than by external ones. A brand that only solves the external problem wins a transaction. A brand that addresses all three wins loyalty.

## The Structure

**1. Character (0–10%)**
Establish who the customer is and what they want. Not what the brand does — what the customer desires. This is the entry point: the customer sees themselves in the story immediately.

Key principle: The customer is the hero. The brand is never the hero. The moment the brand's story begins with "we are..." rather than "you want...", the framework has been violated.

*Mechanism: immediate identification — the customer recognizes themselves and becomes engaged.*

**2. Problem (10–30%) — Three Layers**
The problem has three distinct levels, all of which must be addressed:

**External Problem** — the practical, surface-level challenge. This is what the product literally solves.
Example: "Your scheduling software doesn't integrate with your CRM."

**Internal Problem** — the emotional or identity-level pain the external problem creates. This is what keeps the customer up at night, the feeling they have when the external problem persists.
Example: "You feel like you're losing control of your team's time and can't trust the data you're presenting to leadership."

**Philosophical Problem** — the worldview or belief about what is wrong with the world. This is the layer where the brand takes a position.
Example: "People shouldn't have to spend their most productive hours managing spreadsheets instead of doing meaningful work."

Most brands address only the external problem. The internal and philosophical layers are where emotional resonance lives. A customer who feels understood at all three levels will trust the brand in a way that external-problem-only solutions cannot achieve.

*Mechanism: the three-layer problem creates recognition at the surface, empathy at the emotional level, and alignment at the values level — all three are necessary for deep customer loyalty.*

**3. Guide (30–45%)**
The brand enters as the guide — not the hero. The guide has two mandatory qualities:
- **Empathy:** "We understand what you're going through." (Addresses the internal problem.)
- **Authority:** "And we know how to help." (Establishes credibility without arrogance.)

The guide never positions themselves as the star. They position themselves as the wise, experienced helper who has seen this problem before and has a proven path through it.

Key markers of a guide: testimonials, data, credentials, years of experience, client logos — all evidence of capability, not self-congratulation.

*Mechanism: the guide position creates trust by combining emotional resonance (empathy) with demonstrated competence (authority), without the brand taking over the customer's story.*

**4. Plan (45–65%)**
The guide provides a simple, clear plan that shows the customer how to use the product or service to solve their problem. Maximum three steps. The plan must feel achievable — if it looks complicated, the customer's brain reads it as risk and disengages.

The plan answers: "If I work with you, what do I actually do?"

Note: The plan is not the full onboarding process. It is a simplified representation that makes the path feel manageable. Three steps, labeled clearly, that transform the unknown into the knowable.

*Mechanism: reduces perceived risk — the customer's brain needs to see a path before it will commit to walking it.*

**5. Call to Action (65–70%)**
One specific, direct ask. Not multiple options — one. The CTA must be clear about what the customer does next: Schedule a demo. Start your free trial. Download the guide. Book a call.

There are two types of CTA:
- **Direct CTA:** The primary action (buy, subscribe, schedule)
- **Transitional CTA:** A lower-commitment action for customers not yet ready to buy (download a resource, attend a webinar)

Both types should always be present in marketing materials. The direct CTA converts ready buyers; the transitional CTA nurtures hesitant ones.

*Mechanism: decision point — the plan has reduced fear, now a specific ask channels the customer's readiness into action.*

**6. Avoiding Failure (70–80%)**
Show what happens if the customer does nothing — the cost of inaction. This must be specific and credible, not exaggerated. The goal is to activate the customer's loss aversion without creating paralyzing fear.

Key principle: Never dwell here. This section should be brief and factual. Its job is to make inaction feel real, not to frighten.

*Mechanism: activates loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky) — the brain weighs potential losses more heavily than potential gains of equal magnitude. A brief, accurate statement of what the customer loses by not acting is more motivating than an extended description of what they gain.*

**7. Achieving Success (80–100%)**
Paint a specific, vivid picture of the customer's life after the problem is solved. Not the brand's success — the customer's success. This is their story's ending.

The success image should address all three problem layers:
- External: the practical outcome ("Your team will close 30% more deals.")
- Internal: the emotional resolution ("You'll walk into Monday's board meeting confident.")
- Philosophical: the world as it should be ("Your team will spend their time on work that matters.")

*Mechanism: aspirational identity — the customer's brain begins to associate the brand with the version of themselves they want to become. This is the deepest form of brand loyalty.*

## Visual Shape

StoryBrand follows a narrative arc from problem to resolution, with the brand positioned not at the center but at the side — as enabler, not hero.

```
Customer's
Fortune
  |                                        Achieving
  |                                        Success *
  |                            CTA        *
  |                  Plan    *          *
  |          Guide *       *          *
  |       *  (empathy)   *          *
  |     * (problem       * Avoiding Failure
  |   * identified)
  | * (Character — customer wants something)
  +--------------------------------------------> Time
```

The brand appears as a supporting actor (Guide) while the customer's arc drives the narrative. The brand's growth follows the customer's — the brand succeeds because the customer succeeds, not the other way around.

## Psychological Mechanism

StoryBrand works because of identification and narrative transportation. When customers see themselves accurately reflected (Character + three-layer Problem), they experience narrative transportation — they are pulled into the story and begin to evaluate the brand within the story's logic rather than through external skepticism. Research by Green and Brock (2000) shows that transported audiences are more likely to update their beliefs in the direction of the narrative.

The three-layer problem structure draws on Maslow's hierarchy applied to purchasing decisions. External problems are practical needs (lower hierarchy). Internal problems are belonging, esteem, and identity needs (middle hierarchy). Philosophical problems are self-actualization and meaning needs (upper hierarchy). Customers who feel a brand understands them at all three levels form emotional brand loyalty that price competition cannot easily erode.

The guide's dual requirement (empathy + authority) works because trust requires both dimensions. Empathy alone produces sympathy without credibility. Authority alone produces credibility without warmth. Research on persuasion (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986) shows that source credibility depends on both perceived competence and perceived goodwill. The guide position is precisely engineered to satisfy both requirements simultaneously.

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

**USE when:**
- The audience is an external customer, prospect, or buyer
- The goal is to sell, inform, or onboard
- The medium is marketing copy, a sales deck, a website, or a product email
- The customer does not yet understand why they need the product
- The brand needs to position against confusion in a crowded market
- The relationship is new and trust has not yet been established

**DO NOT USE when:**
- The audience is internal (employees, executives) — they need Minto or Sparkline, not a hero arc
- The goal is to inspire internal alignment or organizational change
- The audience is analytical and will resist emotional structure (use Scientific Method)
- The medium is a conference talk to peers who are not your customers
- The brand needs to be the credible expert (not guide) — pure thought leadership contexts

## The Stages in Practice

**Scenario:** Homepage copy and 5-email nurture sequence for a project management SaaS product targeting marketing team leads at mid-market companies.

---

**Homepage copy (above fold)**

**CHARACTER + EXTERNAL PROBLEM:**
"Your marketing team is doing great work. But you're losing track of it."

**INTERNAL PROBLEM (sub-headline):**
"When campaigns slip through the cracks, you're the one explaining why — not the tools."

**GUIDE (empathy + authority):**
"We've worked with 3,000 marketing teams. We know the feeling. And we built the way out."

**PLAN (3 steps):**
1. Connect your tools in 10 minutes
2. Set campaign milestones once
3. Ship on time — every time

**DIRECT CTA:** Start your free trial
**TRANSITIONAL CTA:** See how it works (demo video)

**AVOIDING FAILURE:**
"Without a single source of truth, campaigns miss deadlines, budgets slip, and you spend your best hours in status meetings instead of doing the work."

**ACHIEVING SUCCESS:**
"With [Product], your team hits every launch date. Your manager sees it. You stop firefighting and start building campaigns you're proud of."

---

**5-Email Nurture Sequence**

**Email 1 — Character + Problem:**
Subject: "Does this sound familiar?"
Body: Describe a specific scenario of campaign chaos — the team lead staying late to update a spreadsheet while a campaign is slipping. Internal problem framing: "The worst part isn't the late night. It's the feeling that you're not in control of your own team's work."

**Email 2 — Guide (Empathy):**
Subject: "We've seen this before"
Body: Share a brief story of a customer who was in the same situation. Lead with empathy: "They weren't failing because they weren't working hard enough. They were failing because the system didn't support the work."

**Email 3 — Plan:**
Subject: "Here's exactly what happens when you start"
Body: Walk through the 3-step onboarding. Make each step feel achievable. Include a 2-minute video showing the setup. Transitional CTA: Watch the setup video.

**Email 4 — Avoiding Failure:**
Subject: "The cost of another quarter like this one"
Body: Specific calculation of time lost to status meetings, missed deadlines, and manual tracking. Brief, factual, no exaggeration. End with the direct CTA.

**Email 5 — Achieving Success:**
Subject: "What your team looks like in 90 days"
Body: Testimonial from a customer who has been using the product for 90 days. Use their specific results (launches on time, status meeting eliminated, manager visibility). End with the direct CTA and a deadline or incentive.

## Common Mistakes

- **Making the brand the hero.** The most common StoryBrand error. "We are a leading provider of..." is brand-as-hero. "Your team deserves tools that keep up with them..." is customer-as-hero. If the copy starts with "we" or "our," read it again. The customer must appear in the first sentence.

- **Only addressing the external problem.** Websites full of feature lists and specifications are external-problem-only. They win comparisons but lose hearts. The internal and philosophical problem layers are what create the emotional resonance that turns first-time buyers into loyal advocates.

- **A plan with more than three steps.** Every additional step is a cognitive cost. Onboarding has many steps; the plan is a simplified representation of those steps. Find the three moments that matter and name those. The rest goes in the product experience, not the marketing material.

- **Two direct CTAs.** Multiple primary CTAs create decision paralysis. "Get Started / Book a Demo / Download the Guide / Watch a Video" gives the customer four decisions when they need one. Choose one primary CTA and allow only one transitional CTA alongside it.

- **Avoiding Failure that is exaggerated.** If the "cost of inaction" section is overwrought, it reads as fearmongering and damages trust. One or two specific, accurate consequences is enough. The section should feel like a friend telling you something true, not a salesperson exploiting your anxiety.

- **Achieving Success that describes the product, not the customer.** "You'll get access to 50+ templates and real-time collaboration" is a feature description, not a success image. "You'll spend your Friday afternoon on strategy instead of status updates" is a success image. The success section must paint the customer's life, not the product's capabilities.

- **Using StoryBrand for internal communications.** StoryBrand assumes the audience is an external customer who does not yet trust the brand. Employees already have a relationship with the brand and need different things — acknowledgment, direction, vision, and evidence. Sparkline or Minto serve internal audiences more effectively.

## Compression and Scaling

| Content length | Approach |
|---|---|
| **30-second pitch / 1 slide / 1 paragraph** | Character (1 line) + External Problem (1 line) + Guide positioning (1 line) + Direct CTA (1 line). Drop Plan, Avoiding Failure, Achieving Success. |
| **1-page leave-behind / 3-slide deck** | Character + all 3 problem layers + Guide (empathy + authority) + Plan (3 steps) + Direct CTA + 1-line Achieving Success. Drop Avoiding Failure or compress to one sentence. |
| **Website homepage / 10-slide pitch deck** | Full 7 stages. Avoiding Failure = one section or slide, factual and brief. Achieving Success = one full section with testimonial. |
| **5-email nurture / 15-slide sales deck / landing page** | Each stage gets its own dedicated unit. Each email or section has its own CTA. Both Avoiding Failure and Achieving Success are fully developed with customer stories. |

## Combination Notes

**When StoryBrand acts as macro (overall structure):**
StoryBrand is the complete architecture for all customer-facing marketing and sales content. It works at all lengths from a 30-second pitch to a full email nurture sequence.

- **[StoryBrand > Case studies: Vonnegut's Person in Hole]** — The "Achieving Success" section benefits from a customer case study. The Person in Hole shape (normal → trouble → resolution above baseline) maps cleanly onto the customer transformation story.
- **[StoryBrand > Results: Scientific Method]** — When marketing to technical buyers who require evidence of efficacy (not just testimonials), the Achieving Success section can include a mini-Scientific Method argument: hypothesis (the product works) → experiment (A/B test or pilot) → results.

**When StoryBrand acts as micro (inserted within a larger framework):**
- **[Hero's Journey > Customer sections: StoryBrand]** — In a transformation story that includes customer impact as a theme, StoryBrand can structure the sections that describe the customer's experience.
- **[Harmon's Story Circle > Episodes: StoryBrand]** — In an episodic email nurture sequence, each episode can use StoryBrand to keep the customer at the center of the narrative even as the story progresses.

**Incompatible at same level:**
- **StoryBrand + Mission Critical (both macro):** Mission Critical positions the speaker as the protagonist solving a problem. StoryBrand positions the customer as the hero. Combining them at the same level creates an identity conflict about who the hero is. If the audience is external, use StoryBrand. If the ask is internal and verbal, use Mission Critical.

## Variant Notes

- **The 6-stage simplification:** Many practitioners document StoryBrand as having 6 stages by collapsing "Avoiding Failure" and "Achieving Success" into a single "Stakes" stage. This is a simplification that loses the directional motivation. Both stages are always present — failure provides the loss-aversion motivation, success provides the aspirational motivation. Removing either weakens the framework's motivational architecture.

- **BrandScript:** Miller's official companion tool (the "BrandScript") is a one-page template that captures all 7 stages for a brand's core message. It is designed to be completed once and then used as the source of truth for all subsequent marketing communications.

- **SB7 Framework:** StoryBrand is sometimes referred to as the "SB7 Framework" (Story Brand, 7 stages) in marketing circles. Same framework, different label.
