# Mission Critical Script

**Author/Origin:** Jeremy Connell-Waite (IBM), developed and named in IBM communication practice
**Source:** IBM Business Communication training; adapted from classical three-act dramatic structure
**Type:** Micro (insertable within larger frameworks) or Macro (for short, high-pressure verbal situations)
**Best used for:** High-stakes verbal communication where you have limited time, no slides, and a skeptical audience who needs to feel urgency before they will act. The framework for hallway pitches, executive escalations, crisis briefings, and any situation where you are performing the story rather than presenting it.

---

## Origin and Context

Jeremy Connell-Waite is a communication coach and IBM Fellow who developed this framework within IBM's internal communication and leadership training programs. The EXCITE/DISTURB/ASSURE structure is a business adaptation of the classical three-act dramatic arc (setup → confrontation → resolution), with two key adaptations for the business context.

First, the three act names are verbs — not nouns. EXCITE, DISTURB, and ASSURE are things the speaker does to the audience, not sections of content. This naming is intentional: it keeps the speaker focused on the audience's emotional state at each moment, not on what they are saying. The question is never "what goes in this section?" — it is "what must the audience feel after this section?"

Second, the structure is optimized for no-slides verbal delivery. Where many frameworks assume a presentation aid, Mission Critical assumes you have only your voice, your words, and your credibility. It is the framework for the conversation, not the presentation.

The underlying structure is ancient: Aristotle's three elements of persuasion (ethos, pathos, logos) map loosely onto EXCITE (establishing why this matters — pathos), DISTURB (amplifying the stakes — pathos + logos), and ASSURE (providing the credible solution — logos + ethos). The specific naming and business optimization is Connell-Waite's contribution.

## The Structure

**EXCITE (0–25%)**
Establish why this moment matters. Open with the problem, the opportunity, or the insight that makes this conversation necessary. The goal is approach motivation — you want the audience leaning forward, not sitting back.

Key moves in EXCITE:
- Anchor to something the audience already cares about (their metric, their risk, their goal)
- State the problem or opportunity at scale ("this affects 40% of our customers")
- Establish stakes without creating panic — curiosity and concern, not alarm
- Avoid lengthy context-setting; EXCITE should move quickly to its destination

*Mechanism: activates approach motivation — the neurological state in which the audience is oriented toward engagement rather than defense.*

**DISTURB (25–75%)**
Amplify the stakes. Show what happens if the problem is not solved, the opportunity is not seized, or the insight is ignored. This is the longest and most challenging section — it must create genuine urgency without tipping into manipulation or fear-mongering.

Key moves in DISTURB:
- Show the obstacles that stand between the current state and the desired outcome
- Use "what if" framing: "What if we don't address this before Q4?"
- Make the cost of inaction specific and personal to the audience ("your team would...")
- Anticipate and address the primary objection within this section
- Build progressively — each obstacle should increase the sense of urgency

*Mechanism: creates mild threat arousal — the physiological and psychological readiness for action that comes from recognizing a genuine risk. The audience becomes primed to receive and act on the ASSURE section.*

**ASSURE (75–100%)**
Resolve the tension. Provide the solution, the path forward, or the specific ask that will address what EXCITE raised and DISTURB amplified. This section does not need to be long — after the tension of DISTURB, the audience is ready to hear resolution. But it must be specific, credible, and actionable.

Key moves in ASSURE:
- Deliver the solution concisely — this is not the place for lengthy explanation
- Establish why this solution is credible: team capability, precedent, data
- State the ask with precision: amount, timeline, decision, commitment
- Create urgency for action: why now, not later
- End with a question or a direct ask that gives the audience somewhere to go

*Mechanism: tension resolution — the cognitive and physiological release of urgency that has been built through EXCITE and DISTURB. The audience is ready to act because they have been brought to the edge of discomfort and then offered a way forward.*

## Visual Shape

Mission Critical traces a tension arc. The audience's attention and urgency begin at a moderate level (EXCITE raises it), climb sharply (DISTURB pushes it to its peak), and then resolve into action-readiness (ASSURE releases it into a directed channel).

```
Urgency /
Attention
  |               *DISTURB peak*
  |          *                 *
  |       *                     \
  |     *  (EXCITE)               * [ASSURE — resolution]
  |   *
  |  *
  | * [opening — audience's baseline attention]
  +--------------------------------------------> Time
     EXCITE       DISTURB          ASSURE
     (0–25%)      (25–75%)         (75–100%)
```

The asymmetry is deliberate: DISTURB is the longest section because building genuine urgency takes time. A DISTURB section that is rushed feels manipulative. One that is fully developed feels urgent and real.

## Psychological Mechanism

Mission Critical exploits the fight-or-flight arousal arc, redirected toward motivated action. Neuroscience research on approach and avoidance motivation (Gray, 1990; Elliot & Covington, 2001) identifies two distinct motivational systems: the behavioral activation system (approach) and the behavioral inhibition system (avoidance/threat). EXCITE activates the approach system — there is something worth moving toward. DISTURB activates mild threat arousal — there is a cost to not moving. ASSURE resolves both into action by providing a clear, credible path forward.

The critical insight is that DISTURB must not overwhelm. Research on fear appeals (Witte, 1992) shows that high-threat messages produce action only when paired with high-efficacy messages — the audience must believe they can do something about the threat. If DISTURB creates a sense of helplessness, the audience disengages rather than acts. ASSURE must arrive before the audience tips from urgency into resignation.

The verb-form naming (EXCITE, DISTURB, ASSURE) matters because it keeps the speaker's orientation where it belongs: on the audience's state, not on the content. A speaker asking "have I EXCITEd them?" is asking a better question than "have I covered all the context?" The framework is fundamentally audience-centric in a way that SCQA or 3-act labels are not.

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

**USE when:**
- The setting is verbal: a meeting, a hallway conversation, a phone call, a short presentation
- You have 2–15 minutes with a skeptical or busy audience
- No slides are available or appropriate
- The goal is to create urgency and get a commitment or decision
- The audience is senior and will resist lengthy context-setting
- You are making an escalation, a pitch, an ask, or a crisis brief

**DO NOT USE when:**
- The audience needs analytical rigor — they will feel the emotional arc as manipulation (use Scientific Method)
- The content is a long presentation where emotional build alone won't carry the audience (use Sparkline or Save the Cat)
- The audience is a conference or general public where the speaker-as-protagonist structure feels self-serving (use Sparkline or StoryBrand)
- The goal is to educate or teach, not persuade (use Save the Cat)
- The relationship is primarily customer-facing, where the customer must be the hero (use StoryBrand)

## The Stages in Practice

**Scenario:** A Sales VP has 5 minutes in a hallway before a CFO's next meeting. No slides. The VP wants budget approval for an additional sales engineer to close a $2M deal that is stalling.

---

**EXCITE [~1 min]**
"Sarah, I have something I need your eyes on before the quarter closes. We have a $2M deal with Northgate Financial that is 60 days from close. It's the biggest new-logo opportunity we've had this year, and it's at risk — not because of price, not because of competition, but because of bandwidth."

**DISTURB [~2.5 min]**
"Here's what's happening: the technical evaluation is stalled because Marcus is the only sales engineer available, and he is split across 4 other deals this month. Northgate's CTO flagged to our AE that they need a dedicated technical resource for the next 6 weeks or they'll delay the decision to Q1.

A Q1 close means this misses the quarter. That's $2M out of Q4 and into a new fiscal year where we can't predict the competitive landscape. I've also heard informally that Vendor B is making a late push — they heard the deal is moving slowly.

We have a contractor who can step in — experience with enterprise financial services, Northgate-compatible stack, available immediately. The cost is $18K for 6 weeks."

**ASSURE [~1.5 min]**
"I'm asking for $18K against a $2M close. That's a 100:1 return if this closes on schedule. Marcus has already briefed the contractor. Northgate's CTO has indicated he'd welcome the dedicated resource. The risk of not acting is losing the deal or pushing it to Q1. The ask is a simple yes from you today so we can onboard the contractor by Monday. Can I get that?"

## Common Mistakes

- **Making EXCITE too long.** EXCITE should open, establish stakes, and move. If EXCITE runs more than 30% of the total time, the urgency hasn't been created — it's been described. The audience wants to feel urgency, not be told to feel it. State the problem at scale and move to DISTURB.

- **DISTURB that lists without building.** A list of obstacles is not DISTURB. DISTURB must build — each obstacle should increase the sense of stakes. The progression from "this is a problem" to "this is an urgent problem" to "this is an urgent problem that will compound if we don't act" is the job of DISTURB.

- **ASSURE that re-explains the problem.** ASSURE should not re-summarize DISTURB. By the time ASSURE arrives, the audience knows the problem. ASSURE has one job: provide a credible, specific path forward and make the ask. Do not spend ASSURE time justifying the problem you have already established.

- **A vague ask.** "We need to think about this" is not an ASSURE. The ask must be specific: a decision, an amount, a commitment, a meeting. If the audience cannot answer yes or no to the ASSURE, the framework has not been completed.

- **Forgetting the "why now."** The ASSURE section must include a reason to act immediately, not eventually. Without urgency for the ask, the audience can agree in principle and never act. The "why now" can be a deadline, a compounding cost, a competitive window, or a dependency.

- **Using Mission Critical for customer-facing storytelling.** In Mission Critical, the speaker is implicitly the protagonist solving the problem. In customer-facing communication, the customer must be the hero. Using Mission Critical with a customer audience creates the wrong power dynamic — the brand looks like it is the hero coming to rescue the confused customer, which creates resistance. Use StoryBrand instead.

- **Skipping emotional specificity in DISTURB.** "This will impact revenue" is weak DISTURB. "This will impact your Q4 number and put your team's bonus at risk" is DISTURB. The stakes must land personally on the audience, not abstractly on the organization.

## Compression and Scaling

| Content length | Approach |
|---|---|
| **30–60 seconds (elevator pitch)** | EXCITE = one sentence (the problem + why it matters). DISTURB = one sentence (the cost of inaction). ASSURE = one sentence (the ask). |
| **2–5 minutes (hallway conversation)** | EXCITE = 30–45 seconds. DISTURB = 90 seconds–2 minutes (2–3 escalating obstacles). ASSURE = 30–45 seconds (solution + ask). |
| **10–15 minutes (meeting opening)** | Full EXCITE with context + stakes. Full DISTURB with 3 obstacles, objection handling, and "what if" scenarios. Full ASSURE with evidence, team credibility, and specific ask. |
| **Embedded in a longer presentation** | Mission Critical used as the CTA section of a Sparkline or at the opening of a board presentation. EXCITE = the framing hook, DISTURB = the risk slide, ASSURE = the recommendation. |

## Combination Notes

**When Mission Critical acts as macro (overall structure):**
Mission Critical is the complete architecture for short verbal pitches, escalations, crisis briefings, and 5–15 minute high-stakes communications.

**When Mission Critical acts as micro (inserted within a larger framework):**
- **[Sparkline > CTA: Mission Critical]** — When a keynote or all-hands talk includes a specific ask, the Mission Critical structure can drive the CTA section: EXCITE the specific ask, DISTURB the cost of not acting, ASSURE the path to action.
- **[Scientific Method > Recommendation: Mission Critical]** — When presenting findings to an executive who needs urgency as well as evidence, Mission Critical can structure the Conclusion section after the Scientific Method has done the analytical work.
- **[Hero's Journey > Call to Adventure: Mission Critical]** — The Call to Adventure beat in a Hero's Journey narrative can be structured as a Mission Critical: EXCITE why the world needs the hero to act, DISTURB the cost of refusal, ASSURE that the hero is equipped.

**Incompatible at same level:**
- **Mission Critical + StoryBrand (both macro):** Mission Critical positions the speaker as the protagonist solving the problem. StoryBrand positions the customer as the hero and the brand as the guide. These are identity conflicts — using both at the same level creates confusion about who the hero is.
- **Mission Critical + Scientific Method (both macro):** Mission Critical is persuasion-first; Scientific Method is evidence-first with persuasion deliberately subordinated. Combining them at the same level undermines both — the audience either receives manipulated evidence or evidence without urgency.

## Variant Notes

- **EXCITE/DISTURB/ASSURE vs. problem/solution:** Many practitioners simplify Mission Critical to "problem → solution." This loses the DISTURB section entirely — which is the framework's most powerful and most underused component. The three-part structure is not a shorthand for two-part; DISTURB is a distinct act that must be fully developed.

- **Jeremy Connell-Waite vs. Jeremy Donovan:** The framework is sometimes attributed to Jeremy Donovan (author of *How to Deliver a TED Talk*, 2012). Connell-Waite's EXCITE/DISTURB/ASSURE naming is distinct from Donovan's work and originates within IBM's communication practice.

- **Toastmasters "Mission Critical" program:** Toastmasters International uses the term "Mission Critical" for a different program in their Pathways curriculum. This is not the same framework. The EXCITE/DISTURB/ASSURE structure is specific to Connell-Waite's work.
