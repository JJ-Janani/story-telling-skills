# Blake Snyder's Save the Cat Beat Sheet

**Author/Origin:** Blake Snyder, 2005
**Source:** *Save the Cat! The Last Book on Screenwriting You'll Ever Need* (Blake Snyder, 2005)
**Type:** Macro (overall arc for extended presentations, workshops, training, and long-form content)
**Best used for:** Workshops, training sessions, extended keynotes, and any content 45–120 minutes long where the audience's attention and energy must be actively managed across the full runtime. The framework for making an audience stay engaged for a long time and leave feeling transformed, not just informed.

---

## Origin and Context

Blake Snyder was a Hollywood screenwriter who developed the Beat Sheet as a diagnostic and generative tool for feature film scripts. The book *Save the Cat!* (2005) became one of the most widely used screenwriting guides ever published, and the Beat Sheet became the de facto standard for Hollywood story structure analysis. Snyder died in 2009, but the framework continues to be applied — with his permission and by later practitioners — to business presentations, workshops, and keynotes.

The name "Save the Cat" comes from a specific storytelling principle: before you ask an audience to follow a protagonist through difficulty, show the protagonist doing something likable. In screenwriting, the classic example is showing the hero saving a cat (helping something small and helpless) before asking the audience to invest in their larger journey. In business presentations, the equivalent is: before you ask your audience to commit to a hard journey, show them you understand their world and care about them specifically.

**The three-act structure within the 15 beats:**

The 15 beats are not equally distributed — they map onto a four-part structure where the dramatic center (Acts 2A and 2B) is separated by the Midpoint:

| Act | Beats | % Range | Character of the act |
|---|---|---|---|
| Act 1 | Beats 1–5 | 0–25% | Set up the world, establish stakes, commit to the journey |
| Act 2A | Beats 6–9 | 25–50% | Enter the new world, explore its possibilities |
| Act 2B | Beats 10–13 | 50–75% | The new world pushes back; increasing pressure and failure |
| Act 3 | Beats 14–15 | 75–100% | Integration and transformation; close the loop |

The Midpoint (Beat 9) is the hinge between Acts 2A and 2B. It is not a climax — it is a shift in the story's direction. Before the Midpoint, the story is "yes-and" (the protagonist is making progress). After the Midpoint, the story becomes "no-but" (every step forward is met with a harder setback).

## The Structure — All 15 Beats

**Beat 1: Opening Image (0–2%)**
The very first image or statement of the content. It establishes the "before" state — the world as it is, the protagonist as they are, the problem as it exists. The Opening Image and the Final Image must mirror each other: the contrast between them is the measure of the story's transformation.
*Mechanism: sets the baseline; the audience unconsciously files this image as "this is what things are like before."*

**Beat 2: Theme Stated (2–5%)**
Somewhere in the opening, a character (or the presenter) states the theme — what this story is really about at its deepest level. Not the topic ("this is about data") but the truth ("the data is only as good as the questions we ask"). Often delivered as a throwaway line that the audience only recognizes in retrospect.
*Mechanism: plants the thematic question the story will answer; creates the intellectual through-line.*

**Beat 3: Setup (5–10%) — including the "Save the Cat" beat**
Establish the world, the protagonist, the existing relationships, and the status quo. Within the Setup, there must be a "Save the Cat" moment: something that makes the audience like or identify with the protagonist before the story asks them to follow the protagonist into difficulty.

**In business presentations:** Before asking the audience to engage with a hard challenge, show them you understand their world. Reference a specific pain they recognize, share a story from your own experience that shows humility, or acknowledge the difficulty of what you are about to ask them to do. This is the Save the Cat beat — it earns the audience's goodwill before you need it.

*Mechanism: establishes identification and goodwill; the audience will follow someone they like much further into difficulty than someone they are merely interested in.*

**Beat 4: Catalyst (10–12%)**
The inciting event — the thing that disrupts the status quo and makes the story necessary. Not a problem to be solved within the existing world; an event that changes the world's rules. In workshops: the shocking statistic, the provocative question, the real example that makes the audience realize the status quo is not working.
*Mechanism: creates the story's central question; the audience needs to know what happens next.*

**Beat 5: Debate (12–25%)**
The protagonist wrestles with the call. Should they engage? What will it cost? Is it worth it? In workshops: structured discussion, a diagnostic exercise, or a reflection prompt that lets the audience wrestle with the challenge themselves before committing to the learning journey.
*Mechanism: earns the Break into Act 2 by making the commitment feel voluntary; the audience who debates is an audience who commits.*

**Beat 6: Break into Act 2 (25–27%)**
The protagonist commits to the journey and enters the new world. This is the point of no return — the old approach is abandoned and the new is adopted. In workshops: the moment where the framework is introduced and the audience begins applying it.
*Mechanism: clear transition; the audience knows the learning journey has officially begun.*

**Beat 7: B-Story (27–30%)**
A secondary storyline or character that carries the theme in a different register — often the emotional or human dimension of what the A-story presents analytically. In workshops: a personal story from the facilitator, a case study of a real person, or a pair-share activity that brings the human dimension into focus.
*Mechanism: provides emotional texture alongside the structural A-story; the theme often becomes clearest in the B-Story.*

**Beat 8: Fun and Games (30–50%)**
The "promise of the premise" — this is why the audience came. The protagonist (or workshop participants) are exploring the new world at its most exciting, most instructive, and most generative. Challenges are present but solvable. In workshops: the core skill-building exercises, the teaching of key frameworks, the practice activities where participants experience early wins.
*Mechanism: delivers the content the audience paid for; keeps energy high through progressive wins.*

**Beat 9: Midpoint (50%) — The Hinge**
The story's central pivot. Two possible configurations:
- **False peak:** An apparent success that conceals a deeper challenge (the audience thinks they've got it, but the real difficulty is ahead)
- **True revelation:** A moment of genuine insight that reorients the protagonist's approach

In workshops: the first significant debrief, the "aha moment" exercise, the moment where the audience checks in on what they have learned — and discovers what they don't yet know. Often a good break point.

After the Midpoint, the tone shifts. Act 2A was exploratory. Act 2B will be pressured.
*Mechanism: resets stakes; separates the easy learning from the hard learning.*

**Beat 10: Bad Guys Close In (50–65%)**
The obstacles multiply and intensify. The protagonist's initial approach proves insufficient. In workshops: introducing the harder edge cases, the complications, the situations where the frameworks don't give easy answers. "What about when...?" questions. The audience is being stretched.
*Mechanism: prevents false confidence; prepares the audience for the most challenging material.*

**Beat 11: All Is Lost (65–70%)**
The lowest point — a moment of genuine failure, doubt, or confrontation with the hardest truth. In workshops: the exercise that exposes a deep assumption the audience has been making; the failure they recognize in themselves; the feedback that lands hard.
*Mechanism: the emotional nadir; after this, the protagonist (and audience) can only go up — but they must earn it.*

**Beat 12: Dark Night of the Soul (70–73%)**
The protagonist sits with the failure. Not a solution yet — a reckoning. In workshops: a reflection exercise, a journaling prompt, a pair conversation about "what does this mean for me?" This beat is often shortened in workshops but should not be eliminated. The audience needs a moment to integrate what the All Is Lost revealed.
*Mechanism: creates the internal space for genuine insight; solutions that arrive too quickly after a failure feel superficial.*

**Beat 13: Break into Act 3 (73–75%)**
The protagonist has a new insight — the answer to the thematic question posed at Beat 2. This is not a new tool; it is a new perspective. In workshops: the reframe, the principle, the "the framework isn't the answer — the question is the answer" moment. The thing that was true from the beginning that the protagonist now finally understands.
*Mechanism: the insight that makes the Finale possible; without this beat, the Finale is just a list of actions.*

**Beat 14: Finale (75–93%)**
The synthesis and application. The protagonist applies everything learned to the central challenge. In workshops: the capstone exercise — participants apply the full skill to a real problem from their own work. The Finale should challenge participants at the highest level they have reached during the session.
*Mechanism: integration — the learning becomes real when it is applied to a problem the audience actually has.*

**Beat 15: Final Image (93–100%)**
The closing image that mirrors the Opening Image. The contrast between them is the story's transformation made visible. In workshops: show the "before" from Beat 1 and rewrite it using everything learned. The audience sees themselves — transformed.
*Mechanism: closes the loop; the transformation is made concrete and visible.*

## Visual Shape

Save the Cat traces a dual-peak arc. The first peak is the Midpoint (apparent success / revelation). The story then descends through All Is Lost before rising sharply through the Finale to the Final Image — which should arrive at a point above the story's Opening Image.

## Psychological Mechanism

Save the Cat works because it manages audience attention and energy across a long arc through anticipation management and strategic emotional contrast.

The beat sheet's precision reflects a fundamental insight from narrative neuroscience: audiences learn to recognize and anticipate structural patterns, and this anticipation creates engagement. When an audience senses that the Midpoint is approaching, they lean in. When All Is Lost arrives, it lands harder because the Midpoint win set up the contrast. The Final Image is satisfying precisely because it mirrors the Opening Image — the audience's brain completes a pattern it recognized was incomplete.

The Save the Cat beat (within the Setup) works because of the reciprocity principle (Cialdini). An audience that has received something from the presenter — genuine acknowledgment, a moment of recognition, an act of service — feels obligated to give their attention in return. This is not manipulation; it is the correct order of the relationship. The presenter who earns the audience's trust before asking for their commitment will carry that audience much further than one who demands attention without earning it.

The All Is Lost / Dark Night of the Soul sequence is frequently cut in business presentations because it feels uncomfortable. This is a mistake. Research on the peak-end rule (Kahneman & Riis, 2005) shows that the emotional low point of an experience is weighted heavily in memory — but so is the resolution that follows. A presentation that never has a genuine low point lacks the contrast that makes the Final Image feel like an arrival.

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

**USE when:**
- The content is 45–120 minutes long
- The audience needs to stay engaged and energized across the full session
- The goal is to teach, transform, or create lasting behavior change
- The medium is a live session: workshop, training, extended keynote
- The audience needs to DO something, not just hear something
- You want the audience to leave feeling genuinely different, not just informed

**DO NOT USE when:**
- Time is under 30 minutes (too many beats to develop properly)
- The goal is to get a quick decision or approval (use Minto or Mission Critical)
- The content is analytical and needs to stay in data/evidence mode throughout
- The audience is senior executives who have little tolerance for extended narrative arcs
- The session is primarily informational with no skill-building or transformation goal

## The Stages in Practice

**Scenario:** A 45-minute conference talk on leading data-driven decisions in organizations where gut-feel culture is dominant.

---

**Beat 1 — Opening Image [0:00–0:01]**
Show a slide: a beautifully designed dashboard with 47 metrics, all green. "This is what success looks like in most organizations. And it's one of the most dangerous things in a boardroom."

**Beat 2 — Theme Stated [0:01–0:03]**
"The problem isn't that we don't have data. The problem is that we've learned to use data to confirm what we already believe. Today is about unlearning that."

**Beat 3 — Setup + Save the Cat [0:03–0:07]**
Share a story of a decision you personally got wrong despite having the data — a moment of intellectual honesty. The audience sees that you are not here to lecture but to share something you learned the hard way.

**Beat 4 — Catalyst [0:07–0:09]**
"Research from Harvard Business School found that in organizations with strong 'data culture,' 61% of analysts reported having been asked to reverse-engineer a conclusion their executive had already decided on. This is not a people problem. It is a system problem."

**Beat 5 — Debate [0:09–0:18]**
Group exercise: pairs discuss a recent decision in their own organization where the conclusion was known before the data was gathered. What happened? 6 minutes in pairs, 3 minutes share-out.

**Beat 6 — Break into Act 2 [0:18–0:20]**
"Here's the good news. There is a three-part system that changes how your team generates the question before it touches the data. It's not a dashboard upgrade. It's a protocol change."

**Beat 7 — B-Story [0:20–0:23]**
Brief case study: a team of 6 analysts who introduced a "pre-mortem protocol" and what changed in their relationship with their leadership team over 6 months.

**Beat 8 — Fun and Games [0:23–0:33]**
Teach the three-part protocol step by step, with examples at each step. Pairs practice applying the protocol to a scenario. Quick debrief.

**Beat 9 — Midpoint [0:33–0:35]**
"You've got the protocol. And most of you are probably thinking: this would work if leadership would let us use it. And that's the real question, isn't it?"

**Beat 10 — Bad Guys Close In [0:35–0:41]**
"How do you introduce a protocol that implicitly challenges the way your senior leader makes decisions? What do you do when the data says one thing and the room says another?" Case study of a real failure. No easy resolution.

**Beat 11 — All Is Lost [0:41–0:43]**
"The honest answer is: sometimes you do everything right and the answer still gets ignored. That is a real thing, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise."

**Beat 12 — Dark Night [0:43–0:44]**
Solo reflection: "Write down: what is the decision in your organization where data would change the answer — if anyone asked the right question?"

**Beat 13 — Break into Act 3 [0:44–0:45]**
"The protocol doesn't change the politics. But it changes what you can say with confidence. And in the long run, confidence is the only currency that matters in a room full of gut-feel decision-makers."

**Beat 14 — Finale [0:45–0:52]**
Participants apply the full protocol to their own real decision from the Dark Night reflection. Pair share: "What is the question your team should be asking?"

**Beat 15 — Final Image [0:52–0:55]**
Return to the slide from Beat 1: the dashboard with 47 green metrics. "This is still what success looks like in most organizations. The difference is that now some of you know what question to ask next."

## Common Mistakes

- **Skipping the "Save the Cat" beat.** Workshops and keynotes that launch directly into content without earning the audience's trust lose engagement within the first 10 minutes. The Save the Cat beat (a moment of genuine acknowledgment, vulnerability, or recognition of the audience's world) is what makes the rest of the session feel like a collaboration rather than a lecture.

- **A weak or vague Catalyst.** The Catalyst must create genuine disruption — not "here's an interesting topic" but "here is evidence that the status quo is not working." A weak Catalyst means the audience never commits to the journey; they remain observers rather than participants.

- **Skipping the Debate.** The Debate earns the Break into Act 2. Participants who wrestle with the challenge before committing to the framework are much more engaged with the content than those who are simply told the answer. Even 5 minutes of structured reflection or pair discussion constitutes a Debate.

- **Treating the Midpoint as a climax.** The Midpoint is a hinge, not an ending. If the facilitator or speaker treats the Midpoint as the high point and begins wrapping up, the session's emotional arc collapses. The Midpoint should feel like arrival at the halfway point, not at the finish line.

- **Cutting the All Is Lost.** This is the most commonly eliminated beat in business sessions because it is uncomfortable. Without it, the Finale has no contrast — it is just "more good stuff." The All Is Lost is what makes the Final Image feel like a genuine transformation rather than an incremental update.

- **A Final Image that doesn't mirror the Opening Image.** The Opening Image should be chosen with the Final Image in mind. If the Opening Image shows a problem, the Final Image should show the resolution of that specific problem. If the Opening Image asks a question, the Final Image should answer it. Mismatched images make the session feel structurally incomplete.

- **Over-scheduling the Fun and Games.** The Fun and Games section can expand to fill any available time if not deliberately constrained. It should occupy approximately 20–25% of total content time — not more. The beats that follow (Midpoint through Finale) need sufficient time to land properly.

## Compression and Scaling

| Content length | Approach |
|---|---|
| **15–20 min talk** | Compress to 7 beats: Opening Image + Theme + Setup+Catalyst (merged) + Break into Act 2 + Fun & Games (core teaching) + All Is Lost (the hardest truth) + Final Image. |
| **30–45 min talk** | 10 beats: Add Debate, Midpoint, and Break into Act 3 to the 7-beat structure. Dark Night compressed to one reflection moment. |
| **60–90 min workshop** | All 15 beats, full treatment. Fun & Games = 20–25 min. Finale = 15 min capstone exercise. Midpoint as break. Each beat transitions with a modality change (teach → practice → reflect). |
| **120 min+ workshop** | All 15 beats expanded. Each beat in Fun & Games becomes a teach→practice→reflect cycle. All Is Lost = a genuinely challenging exercise, not a conceptual statement. Finale = full capstone project. |

## Combination Notes

**When Save the Cat acts as macro (overall structure):**
Save the Cat is the primary architecture for workshops, training sessions, and extended keynotes.

- **[Save the Cat > Teaching sections: Minto]** — Complex analytical content in the Fun & Games section can be structured as a Minto argument: the conclusion of the teaching point delivered first, then the supporting reasons, then the evidence.
- **[Save the Cat > Case studies: Vonnegut's Shapes]** — Stories and case studies within the B-Story, Fun & Games, or Bad Guys Close In beats benefit from a Vonnegut shape (Person in Hole most commonly) to give each story a clear emotional arc.

**When Save the Cat acts as micro:**
Save the Cat is rarely used as a micro structure due to its 15-beat scale. However, in very long multi-day events, a Save the Cat arc can structure one full day's session within a larger program.

**Incompatible at same level:**
- **Save the Cat + Von Stackelberg (both macro):** Both are complete dramatic architectures with overlapping but non-aligned beat structures. Von Stackelberg's 13 beats and Save the Cat's 15 beats hit different rhythm points and create incompatible expectations for the audience.
- **Save the Cat + Hero's Journey (both macro):** Both are full dramatic arc frameworks. For workshop content, Save the Cat is almost always the better choice (more modality flexibility, better energy management). For narrative keynotes without an audience participation element, Hero's Journey may be preferred.

## Variant Notes

- **The screenplay page number equivalents:** In a 110-page screenplay, Snyder specified exact page numbers for each beat. These translate to percentages for non-screenplay formats. The percentages used in this document are derived from Snyder's original page specifications.

- **Save the Cat! Writes a Novel (2018):** Jessica Brody published an adaptation of the framework for novels, with modifications for longer-form narrative. The business communication application described here is closer to the original screenplay framework.

- **"Fun and Games" in business context:** The screenwriting term refers to the "promise of the premise" — the most entertaining section of the story. In business presentations, this maps to the core content delivery — not literally "fun" but the section where the audience gets what they came for. Some practitioners rename this "Core Learning" for business contexts.
