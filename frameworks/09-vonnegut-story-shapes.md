# Kurt Vonnegut's Story Shapes

**Author/Origin:** Kurt Vonnegut, proposed as a master's thesis in anthropology at the University of Chicago (~1947), later formalized in lectures
**Source:** Vonnegut's 1995 commencement address at Rice University; lecture recordings at various universities (1970s–1990s); essay "Here Is a Lesson in Creative Writing" in *A Man Without a Country* (2005)
**Type:** Micro (insertable within sections of larger frameworks) or Macro (for short-form, single-unit content)
**Best used for:** Individual anecdotes, case studies, short-form social posts, risk communication, and any segment of content that needs its own emotional arc without disrupting the larger structure. The framework for when a story is one beat in a bigger presentation, not the whole presentation.

---

## Origin and Context

Kurt Vonnegut proposed his theory of story shapes as a master's thesis in anthropology at the University of Chicago in the late 1940s. The thesis was rejected unanimously by the faculty — who considered the observation too simple to constitute original scholarship. Vonnegut later said it was "the simplest, most elegant idea I ever had." He spent decades delivering it as a recurring lecture, most famously captured in recordings at various universities from the 1970s onward, and the 1995 Rice University commencement address.

The core claim: stories can be mapped on a graph where the x-axis is time and the y-axis is the protagonist's fortune (or happiness). Different shapes of stories produce different emotional experiences in audiences. The shape is not a decorative metaphor — it is the mechanism. What the audience remembers and feels is the emotional arc, not the content. This is why "rags to riches" produces a fundamentally different emotional experience than "tragedy," even if the surface content is similar.

Vonnegut identified at least 8 shapes in his lectures, but 4 are canonical for business communication. This file documents those 4 and provides a decision tool for choosing between them.

**Important note on the name "Boy Meets Girl":** This shape name is dated. For business communication, "Discovery Arc" or "Rise and Fall and Rise" is functionally equivalent. The original name is preserved here for sourcing accuracy.

## The 4 Canonical Shapes

---

### Shape 1: Person in Hole

**Structure:** Normal → Fall → Climb → Resolution (above baseline)

**Vonnegut's description:** "Somebody gets into trouble, gets out of it again. People love that story. They never get tired of it."

**Stage breakdown:**
1. **Normal state (0–20%):** The protagonist is in a stable, acceptable condition. Not perfect, but okay.
2. **Trouble (20–45%):** Something goes wrong. A problem emerges. The fall begins.
3. **Struggle (45–70%):** The protagonist fights to resolve the problem. Progress is not linear.
4. **Resolution (70–90%):** The problem is solved. The protagonist emerges from the hole.
5. **New Normal (90–100%):** The protagonist is back to stability — but slightly better than before. The trouble was not just survived; it was learned from.

**Visual shape:**
```
Fortune
  |                              * New Normal
  |  * Normal                  *
  |         \                 *
  |          *               *
  |           \             *
  |            * (deepest) *  Struggle
  +------------------------------------> Time
```

**Business applications:**
- Product fix announcements ("We had a problem. Here's what we did. Here's where we are now.")
- Customer success stories ("They were struggling. They found us. Here's what changed.")
- Incident post-mortems ("The outage happened. Here's what we did. Here's what we improved.")
- Any "we messed up and we fixed it" communication

---

### Shape 2: Boy Meets Girl (Discovery Arc)

**Structure:** Ordinary → Discovery → Loss → Recovery → Better than Before

**Vonnegut's description:** "Boy meets girl, boy loses girl, boy gets girl. Not necessarily in that gender, obviously."

**Stage breakdown:**
1. **Ordinary (0–15%):** The protagonist is in a normal, unremarkable state.
2. **Discovery (15–35%):** Something wonderful is encountered — a partnership, an insight, an opportunity, a breakthrough.
3. **Loss (35–60%):** The wonderful thing is taken away or threatened. The audience experiences the depth of the discovery through its loss.
4. **Recovery (60–85%):** The protagonist pursues and recovers what was lost — at a higher level of effort and understanding.
5. **Resolution (85–100%):** The protagonist has the wonderful thing — now fully understood, more deeply valued.

**Visual shape:**
```
Fortune
  |          * Discovery                 * Resolution
  |         *                          *
  |   Ordinary*                      *
  |             \                   *
  |              * Loss            *
  |                \              *
  |                 * (lowest)   * Recovery
  +--------------------------------------------> Time
```

**Business applications:**
- Case studies where a client relationship was nearly lost (account churn, nearly lost deal, relationship that required work)
- Product innovation stories (found the insight, nearly shipped wrong, corrected course)
- Partnership stories (great early alignment, went through a difficult period, emerged stronger)
- The "we almost lost them and here's what we learned" narrative

---

### Shape 3: Cinderella

**Structure:** Grief → Good Fortune → Ultimate High → Sudden Loss → Greater Happiness

**Vonnegut's description:** "A very common story is one of continuous good fortune. But those stories are actually more structurally interesting than they look. The Cinderella story is the one where things keep getting better and better and then there's a terrible reversal and then things get very, very good indeed."

**Stage breakdown:**
1. **Grief / Low Start (0–15%):** The protagonist begins in deprivation, difficulty, or disadvantage.
2. **Good Fortune (15–40%):** Things begin to improve — progress, recognition, opportunity arrives.
3. **Ultimate High (40–50%):** The peak — the ball, the breakthrough, the moment of maximum good fortune.
4. **Sudden Loss (50–70%):** The high is snatched away. Midnight strikes. The clock runs out. The deal falls through.
5. **Hopeful Perseverance (70–80%):** The protagonist holds onto what remains — the glass slipper, the insight, the core of what was good.
6. **Greater Happiness (80–100%):** A resolution that is higher than the Ultimate High — because it is earned and permanent, not lucky and temporary.

**Visual shape:**
```
Fortune
  |                                         * Greater Happiness
  |               Ultimate *             *
  |           Good Fortune  *  High   *
  |          *                  *   *
  |   Grief *                     * Sudden Loss
  |  *                           *   \
  | *                                 * Perseverance
  +--------------------------------------------> Time
```

**Business applications:**
- Company founding stories (started with nothing, found traction, nearly lost it, built something lasting)
- Product launch stories (built something exciting, launch went wrong, persisted and succeeded)
- Team success stories with a "we almost made it, then it collapsed, then we actually made it" arc
- Individual career/leadership stories with a setback-and-recovery structure

---

### Shape 4: Bad to Worse

**Structure:** Awful → Worse → Worse → Worse (no recovery)

**Vonnegut's description:** "Another popular story is 'man in a hole' but there is no recovery. This is almost universally considered to be the most powerful story shape of all, because it describes the actual experience of many people's lives."

**Stage breakdown:**
1. **Difficult start (0–20%):** The protagonist begins in a genuinely hard situation.
2. **Escalating deterioration (20–100%):** Each development makes the situation worse. There is no recovery. The endpoint is below the starting point.

**Visual shape:**
```
Fortune
  | * Difficult start
  |  *
  |   *
  |    *
  |     *
  |      * Worse
  |       \
  |        * Even Worse
  +--------------------------------------------> Time
```

**Business applications:**
- Risk escalation communications ("Here is what happens if we don't act — and here's what it looks like 6 months from now, and a year from now")
- Competitive threat narratives ("Our market share last quarter. This quarter. Next quarter if the trend continues.")
- Security and compliance scenarios ("One breach. Then the regulatory notice. Then the class action.")
- The "here is the cost of inaction" section of any Mission Critical or Sparkline

**Important note:** Bad to Worse is the most powerful shape for creating urgency. It is also the easiest to misuse. A Bad to Worse story told without a solution pathway immediately following it leaves the audience in despair rather than action-readiness. In business contexts, Bad to Worse is almost always followed by a different shape (Person in Hole or Cinderella) that provides the resolution. Use it as the DISTURB section of a Mission Critical, not as a standalone communication.

## How to Choose Between Shapes

Use this decision tree to select the right shape for a given story or section.

**Step 1: Where does the story end relative to where it started?**
- Ends above baseline → Person in Hole, Boy Meets Girl, or Cinderella
- Ends at baseline → Person in Hole (minimal recovery)
- Ends below baseline → Bad to Worse

**Step 2: Does the story include a loss of something valuable that was found?**
- Yes, something good was found and then lost → Boy Meets Girl or Cinderella
- No discovery-and-loss structure → Person in Hole

**Step 3: Does the story begin in deprivation/disadvantage?**
- Yes, begins from a low starting point → Cinderella
- No, begins from a normal or stable state → Person in Hole

**Step 4: What is the primary emotional goal?**
- Create urgency and fear of inaction → Bad to Worse
- Create empathy and recognition → Person in Hole (audience has been in the hole too)
- Create wonder and the fear of losing something good → Boy Meets Girl
- Create inspiration through perseverance → Cinderella

**Quick selection table:**

| Story starts | Story has discovery-then-loss | Primary goal | → Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | No | Empathy / recognition | Person in Hole |
| Normal | Yes | Wonder / loss-fear | Boy Meets Girl |
| Low / deprived | Yes | Inspiration / perseverance | Cinderella |
| Any | No | Urgency / fear of inaction | Bad to Worse |

## Visual Shape

(Individual shapes are diagrammed above in each section.)

The meta-shape: all four canonical Vonnegut shapes are variations on a happiness-over-time graph. The x-axis is always time. The y-axis is always fortune (happiness, wellbeing, success — context determines the specific measure). The shape of the line between opening and closing is the framework.

## Psychological Mechanism

Vonnegut's theory works because of the peak-end rule (Kahneman & Riis, 2005): humans evaluate experiences based on the emotional peak (the highest or lowest moment) and the final moment — not the average across the experience. This means that two stories with identical content but different shapes produce different emotional memories.

A Person in Hole story that ends slightly above baseline will be remembered more positively than one that ends exactly at baseline — the final moment matters disproportionately. A Bad to Worse story that ends at the lowest point will be remembered as entirely negative, even if the middle contained good news. The emotional arc is the memory, not the content.

The shapes also work because emotional forecasting is how humans evaluate decisions. When an audience considers whether to act, they imagine the shape of their future — they project themselves into a story. Bad to Worse projects them into a deteriorating arc, which creates loss aversion. Cinderella projects them into an aspiration arc, which creates approach motivation. Person in Hole projects them into a recovery arc, which creates self-efficacy ("I have been in this hole before and I got out"). Choosing the right shape for your audience's decision context is choosing the right emotional forecast.

The simplicity of Vonnegut's framework is not a limitation — it is the source of its power. Complex emotional arcs are built from combinations of simple shapes. A Hero's Journey is roughly Cinderella (initial disadvantage → good fortune at the Reward) followed by a Bad to Worse (Road Back → Ordeal) followed by a Person in Hole (recovery → Return with Elixir). Understanding the simple shapes makes the complex ones more controllable.

## When to Use / When NOT to Use

**USE when:**
- You need a story to occupy one section or beat within a larger framework
- The content is a case study, an anecdote, or a short illustrative story
- The medium is social media, email, or a brief spoken story (under 5 minutes)
- You need to create urgency without a full EXCITE/DISTURB/ASSURE arc (use Bad to Worse)
- You are inserting a human story into an analytical presentation

**DO NOT USE when:**
- The content is the primary structure of a 20+ minute presentation (use Sparkline, Hero's Journey, or Save the Cat)
- The story requires a complex multi-character arc that a single shape cannot hold
- Bad to Worse is used as the complete communication without a following resolution — this leaves audiences in despair

## The Shapes in Practice — Four Short Examples

---

**Shape 1 — Person in Hole: Product fix announcement**

"Last Tuesday, a caching error in our API caused 23% of users to see stale data for 4 hours. We identified it at 9:47 AM, rolled back the deployment at 10:12 AM, and restored correct data to all affected users by 11:30 AM. We've added a cache invalidation test to our deployment checklist and conducted a full audit of other cached endpoints. This will not happen the same way again."

---

**Shape 2 — Boy Meets Girl: Client relationship case study**

"When we first partnered with Meridian Health in 2021, we thought we understood their problem. By month 3, it was clear we didn't. The relationship nearly ended in April when we delivered a product that solved the problem we assumed they had, not the one they actually had. We spent 30 days doing what we should have done at the start — listening. What we built in month 5 was different in almost every way from what we'd built in month 3. Meridian has been a partner for 3 years now. The first version of the product taught us more than any customer discovery interview ever had."

---

**Shape 3 — Cinderella: Company founding story**

"We started with two laptops and a shared Google Doc. Our first product was a consulting pitch dressed up as software. Our second product was technically better and commercially invisible. In November 2019, we signed our first real enterprise contract — $180K, a name-brand client, validation that we had been right to persist. Three months later, COVID shut everything down and the client deferred the contract indefinitely. We spent the pandemic rebuilding the product for remote-first teams — a version no one had asked us to build. When the world opened up again, what we had built was the only thing in the market that did what it did. That client eventually came back. So did 140 others."

---

**Shape 4 — Bad to Worse: Risk escalation communication**

"Today: 3 unpatched servers in the DMZ. Next week, if unaddressed: those servers qualify as the entry point in the NIST audit currently in progress. Next month: the audit finding becomes a remediation requirement with a 60-day clock. Month 3: if the clock is missed, we are out of compliance under our enterprise contracts. 6 of our top 10 customers have the right to terminate on compliance failure."

## Common Mistakes

- **Bad to Worse without a following resolution.** In a business context, Bad to Worse almost always needs to be followed by a different shape. A presentation that ends in Bad to Worse leaves the audience in learned helplessness — they feel the problem is hopeless and therefore do nothing. Follow Bad to Worse with a Person in Hole that shows the recovery path.

- **Person in Hole where the hole isn't deep enough.** If the trouble in a Person in Hole story is mild, the recovery is unimpressive. The hole must be genuinely uncomfortable for the resolution to be satisfying. "We had a small problem and we fixed it" is not a story — it is a bullet point.

- **Cinderella that ends at the Ultimate High.** A Cinderella story that ends at the peak without including the Sudden Loss and Greater Happiness is simply a success story. It lacks the emotional depth that makes Cinderella compelling. The temporary loss is what makes the final happiness feel earned and permanent.

- **Using Boy Meets Girl when the loss is not genuine.** If the "loss" phase of the Boy Meets Girl arc is mild or easily reversible, the story doesn't have the weight the shape requires. The audience must feel that the wonderful thing was genuinely at risk. A low-stakes "we had a bump in the road" is not a Boy Meets Girl — it is a Person in Hole.

- **Choosing the shape after writing the story.** The shape should be chosen before writing. A story written without a clear shape in mind often drifts between shapes — it has the opening of a Cinderella, the middle of a Person in Hole, and the ending of a Boy Meets Girl. The result feels emotionally incoherent even if the content is good.

## Compression and Scaling

| Content length | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Social media post / 30 seconds** | Use the shape's essence: 2–3 sentences capturing the arc. Person in Hole: "Problem. Effort. Resolution." Bad to Worse: "Bad. Worse. Here is what it costs." |
| **1–3 minutes / 1 paragraph** | Use 4 stages of the shape, one sentence each. Emphasis on the low point (Person in Hole: the hole) or the peak (Cinderella: the Ultimate High). |
| **5–10 minutes / 3–5 paragraphs / 2–3 slides** | Full shape with all stages. Each stage gets a paragraph or slide. The peak or nadir gets 2x space. |
| **Embedded in a larger framework** | The shape structures one beat or section. Keep it proportional to its surrounding context — a case study within a Sparkline's What Could Be section should not outgrow the section it illustrates. |

## Combination Notes

**When Vonnegut acts as micro (primary use):**
Vonnegut shapes are most valuable as insertable micro structures within a larger framework.

- **[Sparkline > Case studies: Vonnegut]** — A "What Could Be" section that includes a customer story benefits from a Person in Hole or Cinderella shape. The shape gives the story its own arc without interrupting the Sparkline's larger contrast rhythm.
- **[Save the Cat > B-Story: Vonnegut]** — The B-Story beat in Save the Cat is the human angle. A Vonnegut shape (typically Person in Hole) structures this story efficiently.
- **[Mission Critical > DISTURB: Vonnegut Bad to Worse]** — The DISTURB section of Mission Critical is strengthened by a Bad to Worse shape that makes the cost of inaction concrete and escalating.
- **[Von Stackelberg > Anecdotes: Vonnegut]** — Individual anecdotes within a Von Stackelberg narrative use Vonnegut shapes to give each story its own emotional arc.
- **[Minto > Arguments: Vonnegut]** — Each WHY argument in Minto can include a brief Vonnegut story as evidence (typically Person in Hole).

**When Vonnegut acts as macro:**
For very short-form content (a single email, a social post, a 2-minute spoken story), a Vonnegut shape is the complete structure.

**Incompatible at same level:**
Vonnegut shapes are micro structures by design. Attempting to use a Vonnegut shape as the macro for anything over 10 minutes will result in a story that runs out of structural resolution long before the content is complete.

## Variant Notes

- **The 8-shape question:** Vonnegut identified more than 4 shapes in his lectures — he mentioned 8 in various interviews. The canonical 4 (Person in Hole, Boy Meets Girl, Cinderella, Bad to Worse) are the ones most consistently described and the most practically applicable to business communication. The others (including shapes he called "Hamlet" and "Kafka") are more complex and less transferable to business contexts.

- **The rejected thesis:** Vonnegut's anthropology thesis was rejected at the University of Chicago. He later received an honorary doctorate from the same institution, which he described as being awarded "in recognition of the fact that my thesis was rejected."

- **Emotional fortune vs. narrative fortune:** The y-axis of Vonnegut's shapes measures "good fortune" in the original — but in practice, the most useful measure is whatever metric the story's audience cares about: revenue, wellbeing, capability, relationships. The axis should be labeled for the specific story, not kept abstract.
